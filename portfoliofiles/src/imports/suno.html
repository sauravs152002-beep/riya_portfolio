Blind People's Association

Founded in 1954 as a small Recreation Club for the Blind, BPA India has grown into one of Asia's largest organisations working for people with disabilities, offering services from prevention and education to rehabilitation and employment.

It upholds the rights of persons with disabilities by involving all stakeholders and promoting inclusive, dignified community participation.

Prabhat Education Foundation

Prabhat Education Foundation (Vatva Centre), Ahmedabad — Research on Kids with Down Syndrome. Age group: 3–10 years. Activities observed: learning, playing, physiotherapy, free time, lunch time.

Observing Type: Silent observer / interacted / guided.

🗺️

A tactile map of the entire association was provided, enabling blind individuals to explore the spatial layout through touch and gain an overall understanding of the environment.

🧍

Therapy Aid

A human-figure therapy aid used in JMMT was observed, featuring tactile touchpoints that help blind users identify specific body areas. This enables independent therapy while maintaining privacy.

A completely dark space designed to help sighted people understand the daily challenges faced by visually impaired people — moving without light, relying entirely on sound and touch.

02
Understanding the Condition

Down Syndrome

Down syndrome is a genetic condition that a child is born with. It happens when there is an extra copy of chromosome 21 in the body. This extra chromosome affects how the body and brain grow and work. Because of this, children with Down syndrome develop differently from other children.

1 in 700 Births worldwide
3 Types of Down Syndrome
Most Common

Trisomy 21

Most common type of Down syndrome. Extra chromosome 21 in every cell. Affects how the body and brain develop from the earliest stages.

Structural

Translocation

Extra chromosome 21 is attached to another chromosome. Effects are similar to Trisomy 21 in most developmental aspects.

Variable

Mosaic Down Syndrome

Only some cells have extra chromosome 21. Features can be milder, but vary considerably by child and context.

Impact Areas

How it Affects Children

🧠 Intellectual Disability

Learning takes more time and memory is weak

Most children fall in the mild to moderate range

💬 Speech & Language Difficulty

Speech develops slowly and words are unclear

Understanding is usually better than speaking

👂 Hearing Issues

Many children have hearing problems

This affects sound clarity and speech learning

👁️ Vision Issues

Weak eyesight is common

This makes visual learning and reading harder

👄 Oral Motor Difficulties

Mouth muscles are weak

This affects chewing, swallowing, and speech

⏰ Attention Related Difficulties

Attention span is short and response is slow

This is not always the same as ADHD

♾️ Autism-Like Traits

Seen in some children

Social interaction and communication can be limited

🤲 Motor Coordination Difficulty

Low muscle tone affects movement

This makes writing and balance harder

❤️ Medical Conditions

Some children have heart problems

This leads to low energy and easy tiredness

Field Observations · Day 01

Play Observation

LEGO & Construction Play

Day 01
Observations
  • The child independently built a tall structure using LEGO blocks and identified it as a "house."
  • She maintained her interpretation when questioned and showed reduced verbal response.
  • She preferred working independently and resisted external intervention or modification.
Key Insights

Gives strong personal meaning to what is created and does not like others changing it. If ideas are questioned again and again or not accepted, interest in communication reduces.

Object Handling

Day 01
Observations
  • The child used a hand gesture to indicate opening the container.
  • She poured moong dal onto a tray and adjusted it to make handling easier.
  • She tried different ways to refill the container, using her hands, the lid, and the mat.
  • She stayed focused on the task and ignored unrelated distractions.
Key Insights

The child shows strong goal-oriented persistence and does not give up easily. She demonstrates adaptive problem-solving by changing methods.

Rubber-Based Construction Pieces

Day 01
Observations
  • Children used rubber-based construction pieces in open-ended, self-directed play.
  • Their play included symbolic actions and imitating each other.
  • A mismatched LEGO block was immediately noticed and rejected.
Key Insights

Children use open-ended materials for symbolic and imitative play with peers. They are sensitive to material consistency and notice disruptions quickly.

Dot Circling Activity

Day 01
Observations
  • The child used a marker on a wipeable sheet with printed black dots.
  • Circling a single dot was done easily.
  • Circling multiple dots caused loss of focus and incomplete work.
Key Insights

Children engage better with simple, single-step tasks. Increased task complexity leads to disengagement. Repetitive or demanding instructions can trigger avoidance behaviors.

Textured Sensory Materials

Day 01
Observations
  • Different textured materials were introduced for touch-based interaction.
  • Children explored textures using hands and fingers.
  • Textures encouraged grasping and sustained hand contact.
Key Insights

Textured materials support grip development through repeated touch and pressure. Simple tactile tools encourage engagement without instructions or outcomes.

Sorting, Form & Orientation

Day 01
Observations
  • Larger openings were easier to use than smaller ones.
  • Shape-based slots required rotation and caused some difficulty.
  • Multiple attempts were needed to place objects correctly.
Key Insights

Size and orientation affect ease of interaction. Simple, forgiving openings support better engagement.

Clinical & Developmental Observations

Specialist Sessions

Ability Spectrum & Speech

  • Vocal cord paralysis limits speech for some
  • IQ determines achievable outcomes; <30 IQ considered severe
  • Many children are trainable and suitable for vocational skills
Communication Behaviour
  • Rarely initiate conversations
  • Most kids are shy and hesitate to communicate their needs
  • Speech limited to word sequences, not full sentences
  • Teachers use open-ended questions to encourage communication

Communication is important for expressing their daily needs and problems.

Early Intervention & Development

  • Vocal training begins around 1 year onwards
  • Earlier diagnosis can help in better development
  • Vocal training for basic communication can be done ~6 years of age
  • Early-start children show significantly better communication later
Oral–Motor Development for Speech
  • Blowing & sucking activities strengthen muscles
  • Progression: feather → pencil → whistle → candles
  • Interactive toys (e.g., talking cactus) reinforce vocal attempts

Early intervention dramatically increases expressive ability and independence.

Fine Motor Skills & Writing Readiness

  • Wrist exercises using slit boxes
  • Paper cutting & folding for fine motor control
  • Size-based sorting activities
  • Activities repeated until movement stabilizes

Fine motor stability is a prerequisite for writing and confident expression.

Motivation, Attention & Emotional Regulation

  • Perceived "laziness" slows response
  • Attention span varies by age & personality
  • Lack of motivation is common
  • Pressure often backfires
  • Personal space is respected when resistance increases

Children require comfort zone and engagement to learn effectively. Teachers act as constant interpreters and facilitators, not instructors.

Synthesis

Key Skill Areas Identified

01

Motor Skills

Activities involving building, sorting, grasping, blowing, and handling objects highlighted the importance of fine and gross motor development for independence and doing tasks.

02

Communication

Across multiple observations, children knew more than they could express. Difficulty in verbal expression often led to disengagement, frustration, or misunderstood intent. Clear communication was essential for expressing needs, ideas, and emotions, making it the most impactful skill area.

03

Social Behaviour

Imitation, shared play, and interaction with peers and adults showed the role of social engagement in learning, confidence, and participation in group settings.

While motor and social skills support daily functioning, communication acts as the foundation, enabling children to express understanding, participate meaningfully, and connect with others.

"Communication is the ability to express needs, emotions, intentions, and choices — not just spoken words."

The gap is not in wanting to communicate, but in how communication is expressed and understood.

05
Landscape Analysis

The Communication Methods Landscape

Communication is most effective for children with Down syndrome with an external aid, as it reduces forced body-based communication efforts which in comparison have a higher chance of misinterpretation.

🖼️

Pictures & Symbols (AAC/PECS)

Visual recognition and choice-making align with the strong visual processing abilities in Down syndrome

🔊

Sounds & Vocalizations

Supported speech and vocal reinforcement for basic expressive communication

🤲

Gestures & Facial Expressions

Natural, immediate, and culturally universal — often more reliable than speech

📅

Routine-Based Cues

Predictable sequences create communication opportunities within familiar activities

🤟

Sign Language / Visual Signs

Hand motor skills develop earlier than speech articulation, making sign a reliable bridge

Speech Supported

Children with Down syndrome often experience hypotonia, oral-motor coordination challenges, and slower processing speed, which makes speech production inconsistent even when comprehension is strong.

Picture-Based Communication

Pictures reduce the need for speech production and rely instead on visual recognition and choice-making. This aligns well with the relatively strong visual processing abilities often observed.

Gesture & Sign Supported

Sign-supported communication works well because hand motor skills often develop earlier and more reliably than speech articulation.

Object-Based Communication

Particularly useful when symbolic understanding is limited or inconsistent. Objects are concrete, tactile, and easy to recognize.

Multimodal or AAC

AAC systems can be low-tech (boards, books, physical objects) or high-tech. The technology level is less important than accessibility and adaptability.

User Study

Meet Raina

Raina

Primary User · Design Subject

Context Classroom, Home & shared social spaces
Lives with Parents, Younger Sibling & Grandparents
Communication Gestures, touch-based cues, behavioural signals
Key Trait Avoids verbal initiation even when motivated
Design Scope Child-specific, not a universal solution

Raina demonstrates emerging communication abilities but frequently suppresses her needs, choosing adjustment over expression.

Full-Day Behavioural Observation

Morning Prayer & Exercise

A day at Raina's foundation centre starts with a routine morning prayer and exercise session. She sits with her peer Vishwa and communicates using gestures and small facial expressions. She feels discomfort because her clothes shift and her hair is tied in a ponytail. This is seen through repeated actions where she adjusts her clothes and tries to loosen her hair slightly.

iPad Draw Turn — Passive Expression

Even when there is a clear want, expression stays very passive. Only small hints are given, so the need is often not noticed. In this situation, she tries to ask for her turn to draw on the iPad, but her effort is not understood and is ignored by her classmates.

Idle Time — Thread Necklace Fidgeting

Often fidgets with a thread necklace when there is no activity to do. This signals a need for sensory engagement and stimulation.

Nose Rub → Burst of Happiness

Rubs the nose due to discomfort, but this suddenly changes into a burst of happiness with clapping. Her emotional expressions are immediate but brief — an important cue for designers.

Appreciation & Social Participation

Shows appreciation clearly by clapping and smiling in the classroom. These positive social signals demonstrate her desire and ability to participate in group settings.

Research Synthesis

Insight Clusters

Pattern 01

Passive Communication

Raina is aware of her needs but often does not communicate them clearly, especially in social settings. Small hints are given but frequently go unnoticed. Expression stays very passive — adjustment is chosen over communication. The gap is not in wanting to communicate but in how communication is expressed and understood.

Pattern 02

Sensory & Touch-Based Engagement

Raina heavily relies on gestures, touch-based cues, and behavioural signals. Textured materials, tactile objects and physical feedback engage her effectively. Her play behaviour consistently shows preference for tangible, hands-on interaction over abstract or verbal prompts.

Pattern 03

Hesitation to Initiate

She avoids verbal initiation even when she is motivated to communicate. Shyness and social hesitation prevent her from expressing needs that she clearly possesses. She tends to adjust her behaviour rather than communicate discomfort or desire.

Pattern 04

Interest in Play & Order

Raina demonstrates strong interest in organising, sequencing and colour-based activities. She engages well with game-like elements that provide immediate feedback. Open-ended activities where she has control over the outcome sustain her attention longer.

Design Brief

Personalized Communication Aid for a Child with Down Syndrome

🎯

Core Problem

Raina is aware of her needs but often does not communicate them clearly, especially in social settings, resulting in unmet needs.

💡

Design Opportunity

To support Raina in expressing her needs through a non-verbal, personalised communication aid that builds on her interests and existing behaviours.

🧭

Design Direction

Design a non-verbal communication aid that allows Raina to express needs through interaction rather than speech. Include engaging, game-like elements based on ordering, sequencing, and colour to sustain attention and naturally create moments for communication.

Communication Cue Categories

Things Raina Needs to Express

😣

Discomfort

Often adjusts dress and hair and rubs nose. Discomfort is felt but not spoken. This cue helps express discomfort clearly instead of repeated self-adjusting.

🚶

Move (Personal Space)

Often adjusts dress and hair and rubs nose. This cue helps express the need for personal space or repositioning instead of repeated self-adjusting.

🙇

Sorry

Follows rules and behaves politely but does not use social words. This cue supports basic social interaction and politeness expression.

↔️

More / Less

Understands quantity but waits silently or uses gestures. This cue helps express quantity needs clearly without speech.

✏️

Want

Touches objects instead of asking. Likes writing and organising. This cue helps ask for pen or paper clearly in classroom settings.

🆘

Help (Attention)

Taps table or uses actions to get attention. This cue is often missed. A designated signal helps her call for help without disrupting the class.

Ideation

Concepts Explored

Multiple concepts were explored before arriving at the final direction. Each concept addressed different aspects of Raina's communication and play needs.

C1

Flat Communication Panel with Rotating Disc

A flat board with a rotating disc mechanism allowing the child to dial to a cue. The rotation interaction was found to be engaging and intuitive. However, pointing directly was faster than spinning to find a cue.

C2

Handheld Paddle with Cue Cards

A paddle-shaped tool designed for easy one-handed use. Cue cards could be inserted and changed. This explored the idea of portability and personal ownership of the device.

C3

Game Board with Ordering Activities

A communication aid embedded within a shape-ordering and colour-matching game. This concept merged play directly with communication in a single object — a key inspiration for the final design.

C4

Wearable Attention Caller

A small wearable or carry-on device that allows the child to signal for attention without speech. The rattle/sound mechanism from this concept was carried forward into the final design.

Prototyping Process

Mockups & Iteration

Stage 01

Initial Cardboard Mockup

Size reference for easy handling. First form explorations in cardboard to test scale and ergonomics. The design was held and tested in hand to evaluate grip comfort and weight distribution.

Stage 02

Rotation Mechanism Test

A rotation mechanism to change icons was incorporated and tested. This physical interaction was found to be manageable but slower than direct pointing — leading to the removal of the spinning mechanism in favour of swappable sticker cards.

Stage 03

Front & Back Layout

Placement of different elements on the front. Groove for gripping the product. Size reference of different games on the back. Front and back were defined as distinct functional areas — communication on one side, play on the other.

Insights from Testing

What We Learned from Early Mockups

Grip & Weight

She could hold and shake it, but the handle needed rethinking. Weight distribution made sustained use uncomfortable.

Puzzles

Shapes and arrangements need to be immediately readable. Complexity breaks engagement fast.

Rotating Disc

Spinning the disc to find a cue was fun, but pointing directly is faster and more intuitive for communication.

Feel Matters

It cannot feel like a block. Texture, finish, and form all affect whether she wants to pick it up at all.

Game Longevity

A fixed game gets boring. The product needs built-in scope to swap activities as she grows or loses interest.

Development Prototype

Final Prototype — Board Version

The first fully functional prototype was a rectangular board featuring a communication side and a game side. This was taken to Prabhat Foundation for testing with Raina, generating critical insights that led to the final redesigned SUNO product.

Communication Side

Numbers 1–5 for sequencing, rotating dial card showing a communication cue (e.g., "Go Back"), yes/no buttons (thumbs up/down), and a smiley face emotion indicator. A colour-coded ring spinner on the side provided additional tactile engagement.

The prototype successfully enabled Raina to interact with the communication elements. The thumbs up/down buttons were immediately understood. The rotating cue mechanism worked but needed to be more direct.

Game Side

A puzzle-style game board on the reverse featuring magnetic shape pieces in different colours and sizes — triangles, rectangles, diamonds, circles, and a star. These were designed around Raina's current interests and her doctor's active learning goals, supporting colour and shape recognition tasks.

The game side successfully engaged Raina. She immediately began interacting with the shapes and stacked them correctly. The magnetic attachment needed refinement — shapes should sit flush with the surface rather than protrude.

सुनो

SUNO — Listening Through Play

Derived from the Hindi word for "listen," Suno is a personalised communication aid and play tool for a child with Down syndrome. It enables expression through tactile interaction and gameplay, transforming play into a medium for communication.

01

Tactile communication cues: A small, personalised set of non-verbal cues to express needs without speech.

02

Attention signal: A gentle sound cue for polite attention-seeking without tapping or touch.

03

Engagement through play: Stacking and organising by colour and order to support focus and regulation.

04

Shared understanding: Easy for teachers, parents, and others to understand in everyday settings.

13
Final Design · Flower Form

Two Sides, One Tool

SUNO has two sides — one for communication, one for play. Together they make a single tool Raina can carry, use, and grow with. The final form evolved from the rectangular board into a flower-shaped paddle — more playful, more grippable, and distinctly Raina's own.

🌸

Communication Side

Front — with illustrated cue cards. Five petal-shaped cue cards, each carrying an illustration Raina can point to. The card set is not fixed. As her teachers, parents, or doctor assess her progress, the illustrations can be swapped and updated to reflect what she needs to say next.


The Gujarati labels on each petal (અસ્વસ્થતા / ખસેડો / ભદ6 / માફ કરો / અપો) make the cues accessible to everyone in her everyday environment.

🔔

The Caller — Central Orange Hub

The orange button at the centre is hollow, with metal balls sealed inside. When shaken, it rattles — a small but deliberate signal.


Raina is hesitant to call out for attention on her own. The caller gives her a way to do it without having to raise her voice or interrupt. A shake is enough. It is designed to feel less like asking and more like reaching out.

🎮

Game Side

Back — with shapes and textures. Designed around Raina's current interests and her doctor's active learning goals. The petals hold shapes and textures she arranges, matches, and interacts with — supporting her ongoing colour and shape recognition tasks.


This side detaches by removing 8 screws, making it fully replaceable. A new panel can be fitted as her curriculum evolves.

14
Product Anatomy

Components & Construction

SUNO is constructed from multiple purposefully designed components, each serving a specific function in either communication, play, or the overall durability and adaptability of the product.

🧩 Puzzle Pieces

Shaped and textured pieces for the game side — shapes in multiple colours for matching and sorting activities.

🔩 Screws for Fastening Game Base

8 screws hold the detachable game base in place. Removable to allow the game panel to be replaced as learning goals change.

🧲 Magnets for Puzzle Pieces

Embedded magnets keep puzzle pieces securely in their petal slots while allowing easy removal and reattachment.

🎯 Detachable Game Base

The entire game side is designed to be swapped out. A new panel with different activities can be fitted as Raina grows.

🔔 Caller-Box Cover

The outer shell of the central orange hub. Smooth, rounded, and orange — designed to be immediately recognisable as the attention-calling feature.

⚙️ Caller-Box with Metal Balls

A hollow chamber containing metal balls. When shaken, these produce a gentle rattling sound — Raina's quiet signal for attention.

🟢 Main Body

The central green disc — the structural backbone of SUNO. Holds all components together and defines the overall flower form.

🪢 Loop to Hang the Strap

A small loop allows a carry strap to be attached, so Raina can hang SUNO around her wrist or keep it accessible at all times.

🌸 Flower Base for Stickers

The petal-shaped base layer on the communication side that holds the illustrated cue stickers in place.

🖼️ Stickers for Communication

Five illustrated cue stickers, each depicting a specific need or emotion in Gujarati. Designed to be replaced as Raina's communication vocabulary grows.

15
User Testing

Testing with Raina

Observations During Testing

  • She instantly found it interesting and started playing with it.
  • She tried tapping the numbers as if they had some movement.
  • She enjoyed shaking the product to make noise.
  • She identified the shapes and stacked them correctly.
  • She continuously tapped the shapes as they were not flush with the surface.
  • She found the rotation mechanism easy to work with.
  • She did not instantly understand the symbols for communication (further teaching is necessary).
  • She was trying to swallow the pieces as well.

Further Development Areas

  • Making the number intentionally interactive (tapping)
  • The shapes game needs to be flush with the surface
  • The orientation and length game needs to be clearer
  • Making the relation between signs and the emotions more evident
  • The understanding of yes and no has to be clearer — she was relating it with the icons displayed
  • Making it more handy and light for her
